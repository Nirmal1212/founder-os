# Category narrative & point of view

A narrative reframes the product from "a tool" to "the answer to a shift happening in the world." It's the most powerful GTM asset when you're creating or reframing a category — it makes the problem urgent and your product inevitable.

## The arc (old way → new way)
1. **Name the shift** — a change in the world (technology, behavior, regulation, economics) that's already underway and undeniable.
2. **The old way is breaking** — the incumbent approach was built for the pre-shift world and is now failing in visible, painful ways.
3. **The stakes** — what it costs to keep doing it the old way (rising, urgent). This creates the "why now."
4. **The new way** — the emerging approach the shift demands. Describe the new world, not your product yet.
5. **The product as the bridge** — your product is how a customer crosses from old to new. Now it feels inevitable, not optional.

## Why "why now" matters
Most products fail not on quality but on urgency — the customer agrees it's nice but does nothing. A narrative tied to a real shift answers "why act now instead of next year," which is the actual thing blocking the sale.

## Strong vs. weak narrative
- **Strong**: anchored to a genuine, evidenced shift; makes status-quo feel risky; positions the company as the guide to the new reality. (Think category-defining "the way we do X is fundamentally changing.")
- **Weak**: invented urgency, hype with no real shift, or a narrative the product can't actually deliver on. Customers smell manufactured FOMO.

## When to lead with narrative vs. positioning
- **Crowded, understood category** → lead with sharp positioning/differentiation (narrative is secondary).
- **New or reframed category** → lead with narrative; you're teaching the market a new way to think, and the category is your creation.

## Output
A tight narrative arc (5 beats, a paragraph each at most) — the raw material `founder-os-content` expands into a manifesto/thought-leadership piece and `founder-os-sales` compresses into the opening of the pitch. Keep it evidence-backed; cite the shift's signals.
