# Discovery methods — picking the right one

Match the method to the question. Most discovery fails by using the wrong instrument (e.g. a survey to learn *why*).

## Method → question it answers
| Method | Good for | Weak for | Typical n |
|--------|----------|----------|-----------|
| Generative interview | *Why*, the problem, the job, workarounds | Prevalence, sizing | 5–8 per segment |
| Survey | *How many / how often*, prevalence, prioritization at scale | *Why*, nuance, new problems | 100+ for signal |
| Usability test | *Can they use it*, where they get stuck | Whether they'd want it | 5 per round |
| Analytics / product data | *What they actually do*, drop-off | Intent, the why behind it | all users |
| Sales / support mining | Real objections, recurring pain, language | Representativeness (skews to vocal) | ongoing |
| Diary / longitudinal | Behavior over time, infrequent jobs | Speed | 5–10 |

## The saturation rule
For qualitative interviews, stop when new conversations stop producing new themes — usually 5–8 per distinct segment. More of the *same* segment adds little; a *new* segment needs its own 5–8.

## Recruiting — where discovery quietly breaks
- Screen for the **actual ICP and behavior**, not convenience. A friend who isn't the user pollutes the data.
- Avoid the "talked to whoever replied" bias — vocal, available users skew positive and atypical.
- For B2B, the buyer, the admin, and the end user are different people with different jobs — be explicit about which you need.

## Triangulate
No single source is trusted alone. Pair what users *say* (interviews) with what they *do* (analytics) with what they *buy/abandon* (sales/support). When say and do disagree, do wins.
